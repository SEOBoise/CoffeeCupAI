Monetary Policy · AI Job Loss

The Fed war-gamed three AI futures. Two end in job losses.

Warsh's contingency plans for "super intelligence" include a scenario where the economy grows above trend and unemployment rises anyway, a combination standard macro models do not produce. The data already contains the ingredients for both bad paths.

Coffee Cup AI · November 17, 2026 · 11 min read

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh stood in San Francisco on Monday and did something no central banker has done before: he laid out, in writing, official contingency scenarios for an economy transformed by artificial intelligence. There are three paths: in one, AI lifts productivity and the gains spread widely, while in the other two, unemployment rises. In his own words, one path brings "substantial labor market disruption as some jobs are displaced, rather than complemented, by technology," and another ends with falling equity valuations and "a more notable rise in unemployment."

Warsh insisted the scenarios are "illustrative, not forecasts." But the arithmetic of the list itself is a finding. GDP runs above its long-run average in two of the three scenarios, so on growth the Fed's own scenario space leans positive. Unemployment rises in two of the three, so on jobs it leans the other way. The Federal Reserve is formally planning for a world where AI helps the economy and hurts workers at the same time.

The timing gives the exercise teeth. Inflation is running hot, with producer prices up 6.5% in October from a year earlier, the fastest in almost four years, and markets have priced in a quarter-point rate hike in December. Anthropic prices what could be a $64 billion initial public offering at a $1.4 trillion valuation on Wednesday. The Fed is fighting an overheating driven by AI spending today while drawing up plans for AI-driven job losses tomorrow.

The scenarios

Broad gains, displacement, and disappointment

Warsh opened the Fed's Productivity and Jobs Task Force conference, hosted by the Board and the San Francisco Fed, by saying staff had "begun to evaluate various contingencies, applying stylized, model-based frameworks to generate scenarios," per the published speech text.

The anomaly

Scenario 2 is the one macro models don't allow

Scenario 1 is familiar: technology raises productivity, demand for output rises, workers get pulled along. Scenario 3 is familiar too, the classic bust after an investment mania. Scenario 2 is the odd one. It combines above-trend growth with above-full-employment joblessness, and that pairing violates Okun's law, one of the most stable relationships in macroeconomics, in which growth above trend pulls unemployment down by roughly half a percentage point for every point of extra growth.

The modern economy has produced jobless stretches before, in the recoveries after 1991 and 2001, when output came back but hiring lagged for years, though both followed recessions. Warsh's Scenario 2 happens inside an expansion: the economy grows faster than its long-run average while the job market deteriorates. For that cell of the matrix to exist, AI must substitute for workers rather than complement them, and displaced workers must fail to move into new roles quickly, meaning the Fed is contingency-planning a decoupling of output from employment.

Warsh himself reached for a historical template at the conference, and it is a pointed choice. He asked panelists whether AI might do for services what the China shock did for goods: lower prices while hollowing out a category of work. CNBC's Matt Peterson reported that Warsh suggested AI could produce "its own version of the China shock, this time focused on service workers," with legal and administrative work done more cheaply than a human can do it. The China shock, which followed China's 2001 entry into the World Trade Organization, cost roughly one million manufacturing jobs and 2.4 million jobs overall by one widely cited estimate, and its defining policy lesson was that national averages stayed healthy while specific labor markets collapsed for a decade, an episode famous for policymakers reading the aggregate data too long.

The room

Who is building the contingency plans, and what they disagree about

Venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, Stanford economist Chad Jones, and Xbox CEO Asha Sharma lead the task force. It is one of five task forces Warsh convened after taking the chair, alongside groups on Fed communication, data reliance, balance sheet policy, and inflation frameworks, and it is the one pointed directly at the labor market. The groups deliver findings near year-end, and the FOMC is expected to weigh them, which is the pipeline through which Monday's scenarios become rate decisions.

Andreessen, who closed the day in a fireside chat with Warsh, argued at the event that regulation in healthcare and education blocks the displacement channel altogether: "We've mostly outlawed the kind of job transition that both the utopians and doomers talk about." The Fed is contingency-planning a scenario that its own task force leader says current rules largely prevent. On that view, Scenario 2 arrives only if deregulation opens the door, which makes it a policy variable as much as a technology one. Meanwhile Chad Jones, one of the economists whose work on falling research productivity Warsh cited in the speech, did not speak because he is on leave from Stanford working at Anthropic, during the company's pre-IPO blackout period.

The speech also carried the politics of the moment. Warsh used the term "super intelligence" in place of AI throughout, the vocabulary President Trump's September executive order required federal agencies to adopt. Former Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo, one of the few prominent Democrats at the event, called the wave of anti-data-center ballot measures that passed in the November 3 midterms "a very troubling sign" and said stopping AI "would be very bad for America." Political resistance to data centers is a headwind to exactly the capital investment that Scenario 3 starts with.

The dashboard

Where the data points today, scenario by scenario

Each scenario has observable tripwires. We mapped the latest readings against all three, using labor data through October and market pricing as of November 17. Right now the economy sits between Scenarios 1 and 2, with the displacement mechanism already visible at low intensity, while Scenario 3's preconditions accumulate in plain sight.

Read across, the rows describe an economy that is already partway down the Scenario 2 path without having left Scenario 1. Hiring is soft and young workers in AI-exposed roles are falling further behind, which is the displacement mechanism operating through the hiring door. But layoffs are at historic lows, the four-week average of initial jobless claims at 195,000 is the lowest in the series' history, and payrolls grew by 165,000 in October. Nobody is being fired; the door is just getting narrower. That is also how Goldman Sachs economist Joseph Briggs frames the transition: up to 9% of workers displaced during the AI transition, with slower hiring cutting employment by a further 3%, and the damage accelerating only when the economy weakens.

Scenario 3, meanwhile, is not hypothetical in the way it sounds. Its first clause, "after significant capital investment and stock market gains," describes conditions that already hold. What remains is the trigger: expected returns turning down. The December rate hike markets have priced would land in the middle of that tension, tightening into an investment boom that the Fed is simultaneously modeling as a future source of unemployment.

The pricing

What markets pay for each future

Prediction markets price these outcomes more optimistically than the Fed's enumeration does. On Kalshi, a 2027 recession trades at 22%, and unemployment above 4.5% at the end of 2027 trades at 24%. Roughly one chance in four, against two of three scenarios in the Fed's own framework ending in job losses. The gap between those numbers is either market complacency or Fed conservatism, and the year-end task force reports will not settle which.

Traders did put a price on Monday's speech, if a modest one. The 10-year Treasury yield fell more than two basis points to 5.131% and the two-year fell four basis points to 4.834% as stocks retreated, per CNBC. A Fed chair who says AI might "disappoint" may eventually have less inflation to fight, so the disappointment scenario is marginally bond-positive. Warsh's words moved the long end of the curve on the day he said he was not making predictions.

The sequence

The next twelve weeks decide how much of this becomes policy

The near-term sequence is concrete. The October FOMC minutes land Wednesday, alongside Anthropic's pricing. Retail sales arrive Tuesday. The task force report is expected near year-end, after which the FOMC weighs it against a December hike that markets have already priced. Warsh said Monday that the Fed's role is "neither advocacy nor opposition," and that AI will bear on monetary policy "down the road."

For workers, the actionable lesson is in the mechanism: both bad scenarios run through hiring before they run through layoffs, and the burden lands earliest on early-career workers in AI-exposed roles. The numbers worth watching are the JOLTS hires rate, the four-week claims average, and Stanford's quarterly youth employment updates. The monthly layoff headlines matter less for now, since layoffs are still near historic lows.

For leaders, the fork between Scenario 1 and Scenario 2 turns on the word Warsh used: whether the technology complements workers or displaces them. Adoption choices made company by company are one of the inputs to which side of that fork the economy drifts toward, and the Fed's rate path, shaped by these same scenarios, will set the cost of the capital you spend to make them. The Fed has done its part by writing the scenarios down.
